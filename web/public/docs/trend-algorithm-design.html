The Trend Algorithm

Exactly how the SEPA trend verdict is calculated — every check, threshold, and formula — and how it adjusts the score. Source of truth: shared/trend/trend-template.cjs.

Engine: shared/trend v0 Deterministic · pure No LLM in the decision Date: 2026-06-20
In one paragraph

Given a symbol's daily bars and a benchmark's (SPY), the algorithm computes six boolean checks from price (three moving averages, the 200-day slope, relative strength, and 52-week position) plus a VCP base flag. It maps those to one of three verdicts — aligned (all four "up-core" checks pass), conflicted (any three of four inverse "down-core" checks pass), or neutral — and uses the verdict to scale the reversion engine's existing Setup Quality (×0.6 conflicted, +5 aligned, ×0.85 neutral-with-VCP). It's pure: same inputs → same output, no clock, no network, no mutation.

Why this exists — the problem in plain English

Our scanner is very good at one thing: finding price levels where a stock has bounced before — a "support zone" that held 5, 10, 20 times. It then suggests selling options (collecting premium) on a bet that the level holds again. The bull-put-spread "at support" is the classic version.

The catch: it judges the level, but never the direction the stock is travelling into that level. A support that bounced 20 times looks rock-solid — but if the stock has been grinding down for months, that level is far more likely to finally give way. Selling puts there is catching a falling knife: you pocket a little premium, the floor breaks, and you take a loss many times that premium.

The analogy

It's like rating a stair landing by how many people have stopped there — without noticing the building is on fire and everyone's running down. The landing's track record is irrelevant if the whole structure is heading for the basement. The trend overlay is the smoke detector: it asks "which way is this thing moving?" before you trust the level.

So the overlay adds the missing question. If the stock is in an uptrend into the support, the bounce thesis is backed → endorse. If it's in a downtrend, it's a falling knife → demote. If there's no clear trend → stay neutral. That's the entire job: it doesn't pick trades, it sanity-checks the ones the scanner already likes.

Why not an alternative approach?

There are simpler things we could have done. Each has a real drawback that this design avoids:

AlternativeWhat it would doWhy not (on its own)
Keep reversion only (today)Rank zones by how reliably they held.Trend-blind — it ranks falling knives as top trades. That's the bug we're fixing.
Switch to trend-followingOnly trade strong uptrends.Throws away the whole income engine — you'd never sell premium at support again.
Ask an AI "is this a good trade?"Let an LLM judge each setup.Non-deterministic (different answer each run), can't be audited, and may invent numbers. Fails our consistency bar.
One indicator (e.g. just the 200-day)Demote anything below its 200-day MA.Easily whipsawed — a healthy stock dips below its 200-day in normal pullbacks. One line is noisy.
This overlayReversion + a deterministic, multi-check trend veto.Keeps the proven income engine, adds an auditable filter, and targets the exact failure mode.

What this approach buys us

  • Deterministic & auditable — same inputs always give the same verdict, and you can read exactly which checks fired (no AI black box).
  • Additive, not destructive — the validated reversion score is untouched; we only flag and (later) adjust. Nothing unproven can silently break the scanner.
  • Multi-factor — several independent checks must agree, which filters out the false alarms any single signal produces.
  • Targeted — it's tuned to catch the one thing that actually loses money here (selling support in a downtrend), not to be a general market-prediction model.

How the extra checks improve outcomes

Each check closes a different blind spot, and they reinforce each other:

CheckWhat it catches that the others miss
MA stack (50/150/200 order)The overall structure — stacked up = healthy uptrend; inverted = downtrend.
Price vs stackWhether price is actually riding above its averages, or has already broken down beneath them.
200-day slopeThe slow, reliable trend — and the last thing to turn, so we use it as confirmation, never a trigger.
Relative strength vs SPYWhether the stock is leading or lagging the market — a stock falling while the market rises is a red flag a single price chart hides.
52-week positionNear highs = leader; sitting near the lows = laggard / likely knife.
VCP (base tightening)Whether a big move is coiling — an imminent expansion is the worst moment to sell premium.

Why combining beats any single one: one indicator throws off lots of false alarms (a strong stock can dip below its 200-day in a normal pullback). Requiring three or four to agree means we only demote when multiple independent signals point the same way — far fewer false demotions, and we still catch the genuine knives.

Why the asymmetry (slow to endorse — all four; quick to demote — any three): it's plain risk management. Skipping a decent trade costs you nothing; taking a falling knife costs real money. So the bar to bless a trade is high, and the bar to flag one is low.

What others do — the technical-analysis lineage

None of these checks are invented. They're a synthesis of frameworks that decades of professional technical analysis converged on — we just made them deterministic and pointed them at one job (vetoing premium-selling into a downtrend).

The one-line takeaway

We took the trend signals that professionals already trust, made them deterministic and auditable, and applied them as a veto on the income engine — instead of reinventing a trend model or outsourcing the judgment to an AI.

1. Inputs & contract

computeTrendTemplate({ bars, benchmarkBars, config?, asOf?, benchmarkSymbol?, velocityGuardFired? }) → TrendVerdict
  • bars / benchmarkBars — ascending arrays of { date, open, high, low, close, volume }. Only close drives the trend checks; high/low/volume drive VCP.
  • velocityGuardFired — optional boolean the caller passes in (the reaction engine's approach-velocity guard); used only for the overlap flag. The module never imports the reaction engine.
  • Output: { verdict, checks, down, rs, vcpActive, trendScore, overlap, adjustedSetupQuality(sq), provenance }.

If fewer than minBars (200) bars are supplied, the verdict is forced to neutral — the moving-average stack can't form.

2. The pipeline (step by step)

1
Project closes from bars (read-only — the input is never mutated).
2
Compute moving averages via shared/indicators.sma (reused, never re-implemented): SMA50, SMA150, SMA200, and SMA200 as of 20 bars ago.
3
Compute the trailing-252-bar high and low (52-week range).
4
Compute relative strength vs the benchmark (return ratio + RS-line slope).
5
Evaluate the six up-core checks and the four down-core checks as booleans.
6
Detect the VCP base flag from the last 60 bars.
7
Decide the verdict (aligned / conflicted / neutral) and roll up the weighted trendScore.
8
Expose adjustedSetupQuality(sq) — a pure closure that scales a passed-in Setup Quality by the verdict.

4. Relative strength (how it's computed)

RS compares the symbol to the benchmark two ways — a return ratio (did it outperform?) and an RS-line slope (is that outperformance improving?). Both must agree.

line[i] = symClose[i] / benchClose[i] // the RS line
symRet = sym[last] / sym[last − 63] − 1 // 63-bar ≈ 3-month return
benchRet= bench[last] / bench[last − 63] − 1
ratio = (1 + symRet) / (1 + benchRet) − 1// >0 ⇒ symbol led
rising = line[last] > line[last − 21] // 21-bar ≈ 1-month slope
falling = line[last] < line[last − 21]
  • rsPositive = ratio > 0 && rising — led the market and the lead is widening.
  • rsNegative = ratio < 0 && falling — lagged the market and the gap is widening.

This two-part test is deliberate: a stock can be down vs SPY (ratio<0) but with an RS line that's rising off a bottom — that's not counted as negative. (It's why TLT below reads RS-negative-but-not-falling.)

5. VCP base detection (volatility contraction)

Over the most recent 60 bars, the algorithm looks for a tightening base — successive pullbacks getting shallower while volume dries up. It's a Boolean flag, not a score.

  1. Swing points: bar i (within ±3 of the edges) is a swing high if its high is the max of the ±3 window; a swing low if its low is the min. (vcpSwingWindow = 3.)
  2. Pullback depths: for each High→Low transition, depth = (peak − trough) / peak.
  3. Contracting: need ≥2 pullbacks, and each must be ≤ 0.8 × the previous (vcpContractionRatio).
  4. Volume drying: average volume of the base's second half ≤ 0.9 × the first half (vcpVolumeDryRatio).

vcpActive is true only if both contraction and volume-dry hold. It is not part of the trend score — it acts solely as a neutral-only premium suppressor (§7).

6. Verdict logic

The boundary is deliberately asymmetric — endorsement is expensive, demotion is eager.

aligned

ALL FOUR up-core pass:
maStack ∧ priceAboveStack ∧ ma200Rising ∧ rsPositive

endorse

conflicted

ANY THREE of four down-core pass:
maStackDown, priceBelow200, rsNegative, ma200Falling

demote (falling knife)

neutral

Everything else — including thin history (<200 bars).

no opinion
// the actual decision
upCore        = maStack && priceAboveStack && ma200Rising && rsPositive
downCoreCount = [maStackDown, priceBelow200, rsNegative, ma200Falling].filter(Boolean).length
verdict = upCore            ? 'aligned'
        : downCoreCount >= 3 ? 'conflicted'
        :                     'neutral'

Why 3-of-4 for conflicted? ma200Falling is the slowest signal — the 200-day rolls over after the stack inverts and price breaks down. Requiring only 3 of 4 lets the verdict fire before the 200-day confirms, flagging falling knives early rather than late.

7. Score adjustment — why the score changes

The trend overlay never invents a score. It scales the reversion engine's existing Setup Quality by the verdict. The multipliers are placeholders until the forward-test validates them.

adjustedSetupQuality(sq):
  conflicted           → sq × 0.6     // conflictMultiplier — multiplicative, punishes high-SQ knives hardest
  aligned              → sq + 5       // alignBonus — additive, endorsement can't inflate
  neutral && vcpActive  → sq × 0.85    // vcpNeutralMultiplier — imminent expansion = bad premium entry
  neutral              → sq             // unchanged
  then clamp to [0, 100]
Why multiplicative for conflicted?

A flat penalty under-punishes the most dangerous setups. A multiplier scales with the score — so the highest-Setup-Quality knives (the ones that look most attractive in the list) are discounted hardest. aligned stays additive so a near-100 setup can't be pushed above 100.

8. Trend score (0–100)

Independent of the verdict, a weighted roll-up of the six gating checks gives a continuous trend score. VCP is deliberately excluded (it's a suppressor, not strength), so the six weights renormalize to 100:

CheckWeightRationale
maStack26The stack is the single strongest trend signal.
rsPositive21Outperformance is the key confirmation.
priceAboveStack16Price actually leading the stack.
ma200Rising16Long-term slope up.
near52wHigh11Leadership / proximity to highs.
off52wLow10Clearly off the lows.
Total100

trendScore = Σ weight over the passing checks, rounded, clamped to 0–100. (A clean uptrend = 100; a full downtrend = 0.)

9. Overlap with the velocity guard

The reaction engine already has an approach-velocity guard (it caps Setup Quality when price reaches a zone faster than 3×ATR). A real falling knife can trip both that and this trend overlay — double-charging the same risk. The module measures this without acting on it:

overlap = (verdict === 'conflicted') && velocityGuardFired

velocityGuardFired is computed by the caller as |3-bar net move| > 3 × ATR and passed in. Phase 2 only measures the co-occurrence; whether to soften one penalty when both fire is a later, data-driven decision.

12. Determinism & properties

  • Pure: identical input → byte-identical output; no Date.now(), no network, no global state. asOf defaults to the last bar's own date, never the clock.
  • Non-mutating: the input bars array is never modified (verified by test).
  • No LLM in the decision: the verdict is 100% deterministic. An LLM only narrates a fixed verdict downstream, forbidden from emitting any number or contradicting the direction.
  • Reuses, doesn't reinvent: moving averages come from shared/indicators; the setup quality it scales comes from shared/reaction.
